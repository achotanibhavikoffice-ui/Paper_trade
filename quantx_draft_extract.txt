Abstract—In this work, we developed Quantx, an exchange-
style paper-trading workspace for National Stock Exchange of
India (NSE) equities in which simulated orders are matched
locally against streamed market depth. The system is organized
as four tiers: a React client, a FastAPI service tier, a market-
data decoding and matching tier, and a PostgreSQL ledger with a
Redis quote cache. Its key design principle is a hard boundary be-
tween a market-data plane, which may only read authentication,
instrument and quote data from a provider, and an accounting
plane, which alone creates orders, fills, charges, cash movements
and positions; paper orders are never sent to a provider order
interface. Inside that boundary, the system normalizes prices to
tick size, validates circuit, lot and freeze constraints, consumes
five-level bid and ask depth with partial fills, applies product-
aware brokerage and statutory charges, discards duplicate and
older-timestamp packets, and refuses market orders when depth
is stale. We evaluated the integrated workflow against a last-price,
zero-charge baseline on [MEASURE: N scripted orders across
three scenario profiles], measuring ledger-reconciliation error,
charge error, fill-price deviation from an independently computed
reference, feed-disorder recovery, and per-operation latency at
the median, 95th and 99th percentiles. These results support the
feasibility of boundary-enforced local paper execution for NSE
equity practice; they do not establish fidelity to live exchange
behavior, learning outcomes, or production readiness.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Software that lets a learner practice securities trading must
handle two kinds of information whose truth is owned by
different parties. The first is market data: instrument reference
records, quotes and order-book depth that originate at an
exchange or its data vendor, arrive as a high-rate, time-ordered
stream, and cannot be corrected by the application [1]–[3].
The second is account state: orders, fills, cash, positions
and charges that exist only because the application records
them, and whose consistency the application must guarantee
through transactional storage [4], [5]. A practice environment
is credible only if the first kind of information is treated as
read-only input and the second as an authoritative, auditable
record.
Unconstrained simulators typically blur this separation and
fail in three recurring ways.
• Fill-Model Fragility: Filling an order at the last traded
price ignores finite displayed quantity and the bid–ask
spread, although execution prices depend on how far an
order walks the book and on order size [6]–[9].
• Charge and Product-Rule Blindness: Trading costs
and turnover are first-order determinants of individual
investors’ net performance [10]–[12], and the applicable
charges differ by product (delivery versus intraday) and
by order side, so a simulator that omits per-fill charges
or product rules reports misleading profit and loss.
• Feed-Disorder Fragility: Real streams deliver dupli-
cated, late and out-of-order messages, and processing-
time order is not event-time order [13]–[15]; a simulator
that applies packets in arrival order can let an older quote
overwrite a newer one.
This study evaluates an integrated, boundary-enforced work-
flow rather than any single component in isolation. The
workflow combines server-side-only provider credentials; a
provider boundary that excludes order submission; binary
frame decoding with unit normalization; timestamp-based dis-
carding of older packets and duplicates; a stale-depth guard
for market orders; tick normalization and circuit, lot, freeze,
product and funds checks at admission; cash reservation; idem-
potent order submission; five-level depth matching with partial
fills and IOC cancellation; product-aware per-fill charges;
transactional persistence of orders, fills, ledger entries and
positions; an order-event audit trail; a stress mode that alters
only simulated execution; and read-only What-If scenarios.
A. Problem Statement
Existing treatments address these concerns separately:
market-microstructure models describe how books evolve [3],
[9], cost studies quantify what retail traders pay [12], and
stream-processing and database work address disorder, idem-
potency and atomicity [4], [13], [16]. Used in isolation, each
leaves a gap at the application level. A fill model without a
charge engine yields wrong cash balances; a charge engine
without a stale-data guard can bill fills that should not have
occurred; and a ledger without an enforced provider boundary
risks sending practice orders to a live order interface.
The research and engineering problem is to design a single
execution workflow for NSE equity practice in which (i) mar-

II. R ELATED WORK
A. Market Microstructure and Order-Book Execution
Empirical and theoretical microstructure work explains why
execution price depends on displayed liquidity. Surveys of
limit order books describe the empirical properties of depth,
spread and queue dynamics [3], and stochastic models treat
the book as a queueing system whose state determines short-
horizon execution outcomes [9]. Execution-cost models relate
trade size and speed to price impact [6], equilibrium models
tie price impact to order flow [7], empirical work studies
how markets absorb such flow over time [17]; practitioner
treatments cover the rules and mechanics of exchanges [1].
These works characterize markets and optimal execution; none
specifies the contract of an application that must reproduce
book-walking fills inside a learner’s account while keeping
provider data read-only.
B. Trading Costs and Retail Investor Outcomes
Studies of individual investors report that turnover and
trading costs materially reduce net performance [10], [11],
and that aggregate losses from trading can be large in broad
investor populations [12]. An analysis by the Indian securities
regulator examined profitability of individual traders in the
equity futures-and-options segment [18]; that segment differs
from the cash-equity scope of this paper, and the entry is
flagged for verification in the reference list. These studies
measure outcomes after the fact; they do not supply a practice
environment that applies product-specific charges to every
simulated fill.
C. Experiential Learning and Simulation Fidelity
Experiential learning theory motivates practice with con-
crete feedback before exposure to consequence [19]. Con-
versely, simulated performance can mislead: backtest-based
results are vulnerable to overfitting, and an in-sample profit fig-
ure is not evidence of out-of-sample skill [20]. Reproducibility
guidance asks that computational claims ship with the artifacts
needed to re-run them [21]. We did not perform a systematic
literature search for peer-reviewed evaluations of paper-trading
simulators for Indian equities [VERIFY: conduct and docu-
ment a systematic search before submission]; accordingly, this
paper makes no claim about learning outcomes.
D. Disorder, Idempotency and Transactions in Data-Intensive
Systems
Stream-processing research distinguishes event time from
processing time and shows that out-of-order, unbounded input
must be handled explicitly [13]; logical ordering of events
is a classical distributed-systems concern [14]. Idempotent
processing makes repeated delivery safe [16], and transactional
storage gives atomic, durable state changes [4]; practitioner
syntheses connect these ideas in data-intensive applications
[5]. These principles are general; they are not instantiated for
a trading simulator whose ledger must remain consistent when
quotes are duplicated, late or stale.
Research gap. No work cited here combines, in one eval-
uated workflow, (i) a provider boundary that forbids order
submission, (ii) depth-walking partial fills with product-aware
per-fill charges, and (iii) timestamp-based disorder and stale-
ness handling feeding a transactional ledger. The gap is stated
as three falsifiable hypotheses, tested in Section VII against
a defined baseline B0 (last-price fills, zero charges, arrival-
order packet processing, no idempotency enforcement): H1,
the integrated workflow yields a smaller ledger-reconciliation
error than B0; H2, it yields zero market-order executions on
stale depth and zero overwrites by older-exchange-timestamp
provider quotes, where B0 yields at least one; and H3, repeated

submission of one idempotency key by one account produces
no additional order, fill or ledger entry. Each hypothesis can
fail on the scenario set of Section VII.
III. P ROPOSED SYSTEM AND ARCHITECTURE
Quantx is organized as four tiers; external providers sit
outside the tiers and are reached only through the market-data
tier or the application tier.
• Presentation tier (React 18, TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind
CSS): renders the workspace, the trade ticket and source
badges, subscribes to the application’s authenticated Web-
Socket, and reconnects with backoff.
• Application tier (FastAPI, Pydantic, SQLAlchemy):
exposes a REST interface [22] and a WebSocket [23],
validates requests, hashes passwords with Argon2 [24],
and issues signed session tokens [25] in an HTTP-only
cookie.
• Market-data and matching tier (in-process services):
authenticates to the provider server side, decodes binary
frames, applies ordering and staleness guards, publishes
normalized quotes, runs the local paper matcher, and runs
the intraday square-off worker.
• Persistence tier (PostgreSQL, Redis): PostgreSQL is
authoritative for users, accounts, orders, fills, charges,
positions and ledgers; Redis holds the latest normalized
quote per symbol and an append-only quote stream.
Fig. 1 shows the tiers, the provider boundary and the permitted
data directions. The layering follows established enterprise-
application practice [26]. Password recovery follows published
guidance [27]: single-use tokens are stored only as SHA-256
hashes, expire after 30 min, and are throttled to one request
per minute per account.
A. Separation of Responsibilities
The design enforces one boundary: the market-data plane
versus the accounting plane. The market-data plane may
authenticate to a provider, synchronize instruments, and read
quotes and depth; it may never send an order, hold account
state, or write to the ledger. The accounting plane alone creates
and changes orders, fills, charges, cash movements, positions
and audit events, and it may never treat a snapshot price as
executable depth, display a missing quote as zero, or present
generated practice values as live data. Source labels carried
with each quote (generated practice data, provider snapshot,
or streaming feed) keep this distinction visible to the user.
Provider credentials are used only by the backend and are
never exposed to the browser.
IV. B OUNDARY -E NFORCED MARKET -DATA PLANE
The market-data plane converts provider frames, which
are binary, expressed in paise and subject to duplication
and reordering, into normalized, ordered and labelled quotes
that the accounting plane can trust as read-only input. Its
responsibilities are narrow by design: it authenticates to the
provider, decodes frames, discards what it must discard, caches
what it keeps, and fans quotes out to the matcher and to
browsers. Fig. 2 summarizes the flow.
A. Provider Boundary and Source Labelling
The provider’s authentication, instrument and ephemeral-
key endpoints are called only by the backend with server-held
credentials; the ephemeral key opens the provider’s market-
data WebSocket. The provider’s order interface and order-
event socket are intentionally not used. Each quote carries a
source label: streaming feed, provider snapshot (periodically
refreshed and not an order book), or generated practice data
marked demo. A fresh streamed quote takes precedence over
a generated one for the same symbol, and the generated feed
is rejected in production configuration. The generated feed
produces five bid and five ask levels per symbol every 1.4 s,
with a spread of max{0.025% of last price , 0.01 INR} per
level, quantities drawn uniformly from 100 to 2,200 shares,
and a random-walk price step of at most 0.09%. The paper
engine does not retain provider snapshots, so a snapshot can
never serve as execution depth.
B. Frame Decoding and Unit Normalization
A big-endian decoder handles the provider’s last-traded-
price packet (TC 1) and full packet (TC 3) for cash, derivative
and index segments; the heartbeat packet (TC 10) is ignored.
Quantx subscribes in full mode; the decoder reads ten depth
levels (five bids, then five asks) with quantity, price and order
count, and index packets carry no depth. TC 1 packets and
snapshots therefore yield empty depth lists. Provider prices and
the instrument CSV are in paise, whereas Quantx order prices
are entered and stored in rupees, so every price is converted
at the boundary:

D. Subscription Lifecycle
One upstream market connection is shared by the backend,
and the provider’s limit of 100 instruments is enforced. After
a reconnect, the service resubscribes using a fresh ephemeral
key. A background worker retains subscriptions for symbols
with an open paper order or position even after a browser
closes. A single worker consumes the quote queue and runs
the paper engine’s matching in a thread for each quote.
PostgreSQL advisory locks elect one process to own the
provider session and the intraday square-off worker, while any
number of processes may serve HTTP requests. The browser
socket accepts subscribe and unsubscribe tasks and
emits ready, subscribed, quote and error messages.
V. L OCAL PAPER MATCHING , C HARGES AND LEDGER
The accounting plane turns an accepted order into fills,
charges, cash movements and positions inside one authoritative
store. This section specifies the mechanisms in the order they
execute.
A. Admission and Cash Reservation
Admission proceeds in this order: the instrument must be
tradable (and have a provider token unless the generated feed
is enabled); limit and trigger prices are rounded to the nearest
tick by round(p/tick) × tick rather than rejected; the quantity
must not exceed the freeze quantity and must be a multiple
of the board lot; limit and trigger prices must lie within
the instrument’s circuit band (market orders are not circuit-
checked); market-data availability and freshness are checked

rules (a buy trigger above, and a sell trigger below, the last
price; a stop-limit’s limit price at or beyond its trigger); and
funds or holdings are checked. Rejection returns an HTTP

VI. R UNTIME CONFIGURATION AND STRUCTURED
VALIDATION
Every order passes through four phases, shown in Fig. 3. In
Phase 1 the system authenticates the session, decodes provider
frames and receives the order request. In Phase 2, the rule
gate checks the request against the instrument and account
constraints, the staleness guard and the idempotency key; a
failing request is rejected with a reason and no financial state
changes. In Phase 3 the matcher walks depth and computes
charges. In Phase 4 the result is validated and committed in
one transaction, an audit event is appended, and the updated
state is pushed to the browser.

VIII. D ISCUSSION , LIMITS AND ETHICS
Controlled Synthetic Benchmark. The evaluation uses
scripted replays whose expected outputs come from a script
that encodes the same specification, so it can reveal im-
plementation defects and baseline failures but cannot reveal
specification errors. Scenario counts are small by design, and
conclusions are limited to the scenarios tested; threats to
validity should be read in the usual construct, internal and
external categories [28].
Recorded-Input Limits. The only recording available lacks
exchange timestamps and depth and shows signs of being
generated, so it supports tests of ordering by receipt time and
of B0 but not of depth-walking fidelity; hypothesis H2 on
older-timestamp quotes is therefore testable only after injecting
timestamps or obtaining a timestamped recording.
Code–Documentation Discrepancies. The supplied code
differs from the project documentation in several places.

Posted charges use constants in the paper engine, whereas
the documentation describes environment-configurable ratios
and a default brokerage of zero; a legacy fee module uses
other values (a brokerage of 0.05% for CNC and 0.03% for
MIS capped at INR 20 on every fill, an exchange ratio of
0.0000345, and no SEBI fee). A legacy matcher fills the full
quantity at the last price and applies a 5 × intraday margin
rule, which contradicts the documented statement that margin
mode does not raise buying power, but it cannot be imported
against the current schema. Two stress mechanisms exist,
one of which perturbs prices. The schema’s pending-trigger,
pending, rejected and expired statuses are not set by the live
engine. The application entry point imports the paper engine
and wires it to the feed; it does not import the legacy matcher
or square-off service, although route modules that were not
supplied might.
Static and Dynamic Assumptions. Charge constants, the
stress parameters (spread multiplier 0.005, liquidity factor 0.5)
and the staleness threshold (120 s) are configured values, not
statements of current official schedules or of observed market
behavior. Cash/Margin mode and leverage choices are display
preferences that do not change buying power or validation,
and the auto-liquidation selector is a reminder that does not
alter the fixed backend square-off schedule.
External Dependency. Live quotes require authorized
provider credentials and an active provider session, and the
automated tests do not verify access to the provider, snapshot,
news, mail or help services. A provider snapshot is not an
order book, so execution is possible only where streamed or
generated depth exists; a health response does not by itself
show that the feed is connected.
Single-Process and Scale Limits. The matcher processes
each feed update inside the API process through a bounded
queue of 10,000 quotes, and for every quote it iterates over
all accounts under a process-wide lock using synchronous
database sessions, so cost grows with the number of accounts;
the Redis stream is retained for inspection and future worker
separation rather than used as a distributed work queue,
and one elected process owns the upstream session. The
provider’s 100-instrument limit bounds concurrent subscrip-
tions. Throughput beyond the load levels in Section VII is not
assessed.
Fill-Fidelity Limits. Matching consumes at most five dis-
played levels, depth consumption is not shared across ac-
counts, and when no eligible level exists a marketable order is
filled at the last price, so depth-less feeds degrade toward last-
price execution. Intraday sells are not checked against funds,
market orders are not circuit-checked, no code path expires
DAY orders, and 4,433 of 9,939 NSE cash instruments in the
supplied master carry a board lot of 100, which forces order
quantities to multiples of 100. We make no claim about queue
position, hidden liquidity, the user’s own market impact, or
agreement with real exchange fills; that question is deferred
to Protocol D.
Human and Advisory Decision Boundary. Quantx is
educational software and not a broker or investment adviser.
The optional help assistant cannot submit orders or change
the account, and planned coaching and analytics features are
roadmap items that were not evaluated.
Interpretation Risk. Simulated profit and loss is subject
to the same overfitting and selection concerns as any backtest
[20], and practice without real financial consequence may not
transfer to live behavior. The paper therefore reports feasibility
of the mechanisms and not the educational value of the
product.
Privacy and Security. Provider credentials remain server
side, passwords are hashed, and reset tokens are single use;
these are design properties described in the documentation,
and they were not independently audited.
